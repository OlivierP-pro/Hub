The log S-fBM model

The log Stationary Fractional Brownian Motion (log S-fBM) is a novel stochastic process designed to model financial volatility. It bridges rough and multifractal volatility models by capturing the "roughness" of volatility when the Hurst exponent \( H \) approaches zero. The log S-fBM is particularly useful because it combines the flexibility of fractional Brownian motion with stationary covariance, making it suitable for modeling both short-term and long-term volatility dynamics.

By taking the exponential of the S-fBM process, the resulting measure reflects multifractal properties of asset returns, such as non-trivial scaling behavior and heavy-tailed distributions. This makes the log S-fBM a powerful tool for accurately reproducing empirical features of financial markets, including the power-law behavior of option prices near maturity.

To estimate the parameters of the log S-fBM model, advanced statistical techniques like the Generalized Method of Moments (GMM) are employed. The GMM approach leverages analytical expressions for the second-order moments of the log S-fBM measure and its logarithm, ensuring robust parameter estimation even with small intermittency effects. The methodology is validated through numerical simulations and applied to real-world data, such as daily volatility of individual stocks and market indices.

Key advantages of the log S-fBM include:

  • Flexibility: Unifies rough and multifractal volatility models, enabling analysis across different volatility regimes.
  • Accuracy: Captures key empirical properties of financial volatility, such as scaling behavior and heavy tails.
  • Practicality: Efficient calibration to large datasets using GMM ensures real-world applicability.

Creating a Voting System

Towards a Secure and Verifiable Online Voting System

The 2022 legislative elections marked a significant advancement with the introduction of online voting for French deputies representing overseas constituencies. This option, adopted by more than 77% of voters, highlights the importance for citizens, especially isolated individuals, to be able to cast their votes remotely.

However, the solution implemented via the Scytl platform presents a major limitation: the absence of a mechanism allowing voters to verify that their vote has been counted. Unlike physical voting, where verification is visual, voters must trust a central entity to tally the votes.

Solutions to Ensure Transparency

To address this issue, several approaches have emerged to guarantee the transparency and integrity of elections. Some solutions draw inspiration from cryptocurrencies and integrate blockchain into voting systems. This technology enables complete decentralization by eliminating the need for a central server. However, its use leads to significant costs and energy consumption. For example, the French company V8TE, which relies on an Ethereum blockchain, faces these challenges, limiting the feasibility of such an approach on a large scale.

A More Efficient Alternative

This is why I propose an alternative system based on three key technologies, ensuring the security and verifiability of voting without the energy and financial drawbacks of a public blockchain:

  • Homomorphic encryption: This allows votes to be counted without decrypting each individual choice, thus ensuring ballot confidentiality.
  • Zero-knowledge proofs: These ensure that each vote is valid without revealing its content, enhancing the transparency of the process.
  • An individual verification function: Each voter can confirm that their vote has been correctly counted in the final tally.

This model follows the principles of the Helios project, aiming to expand its application to large-scale elections involving up to a million participants while optimizing the resources needed for its operation.

Beyond securing online voting, such a system could address two major challenges. Firstly, it would provide an alternative for elections where the central entity is deemed unreliable or vulnerable to cyberattacks. Secondly, it would pave the way for a new form of direct democracy. We could imagine deputies, elected officials, or ministers using this mechanism to directly consult public opinion on certain issues, without the risk of interference in the results, unlike centralized systems.

This decentralized model thus represents a promising avenue for enhancing transparency and trust in online voting while avoiding the technical and economic pitfalls associated with the systematic use of blockchain.

scheduling dependent non preemptive task

The problem addressed in the study is how to efficiently schedule tasks that depend on each other, minimizing the total time required to complete them (known as "makespan"). This is a common challenge in many industries, such as manufacturing, computing, and project management. The study evaluated several scheduling methods, including Blevel, Genetic Algorithm, and others, to find the most effective solution. It found that the Blevel approach consistently performed the best, significantly reducing completion times compared to other methods. While the Genetic Algorithm showed potential for further improvement, it was less practical due to high computational costs and variability. Thus, the study concludes that Blevel is currently the optimal solution for minimizing makespan, providing a practical and efficient way for companies to improve task scheduling and increase productivity.

Bayesian Modeling for Defect Prediction

This project aims to explore and analyze the relationship between two key variables in an industrial or inventory management context: the number of days a product has been stored (X) and the number of defects observed on this product (Y). The main objective is to determine whether there is a cause-and-effect relationship between these two variables and to use this knowledge to predict the number of defects based on storage time. To achieve this, we adopt a Bayesian approach, which allows us to model uncertainties in a rigorous and flexible manner, particularly when the number of analyzed products is small (<100).

The main steps include:

  • Statistical modeling: Define a probabilistic model linking X and Y (e.g., a Poisson distribution with a rate dependent on X).
  • Bayesian inference: Estimate the model parameters based on available data.
  • Validation and interpretation: Assess the model's quality and test the hypothesis of a relationship between X and Y.
  • Prediction and decision-making: Use the model to predict the number of defects for new products and define inventory management strategies.
